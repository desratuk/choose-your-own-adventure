# The Stopped Clocks — second-pass editorial review

**Current status:** revised and independently passed. The body preserves the baseline critique; implementation and acceptance are recorded at the end.

**Full spoilers: all routes, puzzles and endings. Baseline critique; implementation status appears at the end.** Reviewed against `stories/stopped-clocks/story.ts` at `8df3b37`, its parent revision, the current story bible and the engine’s actual conditional-text/choice semantics. This is a commissioning and developmental assessment, not another continuity certificate. The route figures below come from engine-rendered source analysis supplied for this review; I did not play these routes in a browser or conduct reader testing. I have not assessed the finished illustrations here, and this is not a claim of licensor approval or an external canon audit.

My audience assumption is a confident younger reader, approximately 10–14, alongside older family and franchise readers. That is an editorial working assumption, not a market requirement supplied by the author. The stated requirements remain an anonymous original protagonist, newcomer accessibility, family adventure, a 15–25 minute run, meaningful costs and an inference-led best ending.

**Commissioning verdict: retain the premise and commission a focused final revision; do not publish this draft unchanged.** It is engaging, recognisably a time-travel adventure and substantially better than the previous version. Its best ideas could support a professional tie-in: a preservation volunteer confronting preservation as imprisonment; a museum displaying your possible failure; a personal recording that matters more than its collectible machine. The remaining gap is principally the reader’s experience of making decisions. The countdown does not consistently communicate what the fiction spends, and several choices either supply the solution or oppose an obviously disastrous action to an obviously workable one. The emotionally important aftermath also receives less attention than the machinery. These need targeted work, not another premise, more endings or a larger cast.

**What the previous pass actually achieved**

The earlier fixes are substantive and should stay. S01 now begins with “three dollars and a button,” giving us an unsuccessful campaign and a person before background history. The cassette is a specific possession with an emotional history, not merely a trade token. The P.S., “Leave the past something to work with,” replaces an explicit spare-wheel instruction with a principle the reader can apply. S23 makes the tested wheel genuinely tempting. Removing the safe-combination guessing game from S13 protects Doc’s competence and avoids a trivia-shaped obstacle.

The repairs to the 1885 routes are particularly valuable. Doc offers a limited concealed escort at S20; E03 now follows the player’s decision to seek an introduction, rather than simply trusting his offered disguise. At S25 he notices the removed wheel immediately and insists on putting it back. The mistake can be corrected where it happened, and E05 follows refusal to repair it. Do not recover difficulty by making Doc neglect these responsibilities again.

S40–S44 now give the volunteer physical work that matters. The exchange of lamp and soldering iron in S41 creates partnership; the setting catch turns the protagonist’s local knowledge into action. Alerting the Authority no longer produces an automatic arrest: the player must cause the broadcast. The distinction between alerting and actually catching Rex is preserved in the aftermath. These are improvements in authorship and agency, not merely repaired flags. My reservations concern the strength of the remaining puzzle and drama, not the decision to introduce them.

**The hook, protagonist and emotional investment**

The opening earns attention quickly. A disappointing afternoon of fundraising is invaded by a century-old letter addressed to you. The instruction to arrive one minute before the familiar stopped time is an excellent small mystery. S02’s train arrival and “Photograph quiet” convert that promise into a striking experience. S05 extends the premise through physical observations rather than another explanation: the water, newspaper and fully committed airborne cat are worth preserving even though this optional page gives no essential inventory item.

The protagonist is sufficiently specific to be a character without requiring a name or gender. Their voluntary work, cassette collection, imagined band and wish to hear the tower tick create a credible teenage perspective. The arc from requesting donations to doing the work, and then accepting responsibility as keeper, is coherent. This is a marked improvement over a visitor holding Doc’s lamp while the franchise hero solves everything.

The remaining emotional weakness is the thinness of the people being saved. S04 mentions the frozen family once. Red becomes the story’s representative citizen, but we know him mainly through sleeping, a joke about the clock and other people’s descriptions. At S31 the guide reduces him to “an authentic vagrant”; at S43 Rex calls him street furniture. The protagonist rejects that reduction, but Red still never gets to answer after being rescued. For a story whose climax asks who gets a say, this absence matters.

One brief restored interaction would carry more weight than another declaration of the theme. Let Red express his own opinion about the newly ticking clock, possibly still disagreeing with the volunteer. Or let the protagonist hear the ordinary family sound they feared losing. This need not become a gratitude ceremony. It would make “You kept looking for the people” in E10 feel lived rather than chiefly summarised. Keep the original character’s restraint; do not solve this by adding a traumatic backstory.

**Doc, Rex and the franchise promise**

Doc is generally an effective companion: quick, enthusiastic, worried about Marty, physically fallible and capable. “Look at my posture. Terrible” at S22 is character comedy arising from time travel, rather than an inserted catchphrase. The false beard and smoke rescue offer family-friendly spectacle. Marty’s absence is explained and leaves room for the original protagonist. There is no need to recruit additional familiar characters simply to resemble a larger ensemble.

Doc nevertheless sometimes becomes the delivery mechanism for instructions. S04 explains the field, bracelet, frozen people and source of the disturbance; S09 then explains the scar, countdown, required device and three components. Those facts matter, but the repeated lecturer-and-questioner arrangement makes the reader feel younger than the best observational prose does. S14’s “Don’t ask me why it’s eighty-eight. It just is” is the weakest example: it closes curiosity with an authorial shrug. A brief impatient or empirical explanation would keep his eccentricity without making him sound uninterested in his own science.

Rex now has a useful thematic motive. “With nobody leaving it” in S42 adds possessiveness to the revenue figure in S33. He has noticed a genuine failure—the council would demolish the tower—and converted it into a justification for owning people. That is more distinctive than simply another bad Tannen. S31’s false consent claim also gives the player something concrete to expose.

His behaviour is still very convenient for the player. He leaves a usable access card, an incriminating log labelled UNREGISTERED JUMP and a ready export function; after confronting his opponents, he accepts an interview that will let one of them speak directly to his audience. Individual conveniences are acceptable adventure compression. Together they make him seem easier to defeat than his control of a century’s security and tourism suggests. The broadcast lure already appeals to money and vanity, so retain it; give him one credible reason to believe he controls that appearance, or a small attempt to manage the interview that the player defeats. A sentence of setup and a beat of resistance can do this without adding a new puzzle or monologue.

The letter, courthouse, train, repeated family bully and past/future contrast supply a strong franchise feel. The museum is where this story becomes its own adventure. The Old West material is more familiar: wanted poster, threatening outlaw, captive in a saloon, disguised rescue. Familiarity is welcome in a tie-in, but that section needs a personal consequence to justify more than recognition. The report makes no claim that any invented temporal mechanism is established screen canon; internal intelligibility is the relevant standard here.

**Prose, scene construction and length**

The prose is readable and often vivid. The train settling with an almost polite hiss, the frozen square that does not notice you, and the retained “summer inside” the cassette make the extraordinary understandable through ordinary experience. The opening is not bloated now. The strongest humour is observational or character-based; the weakest tells us how to judge a choice. S28’s “works about as well as you’d expect” mildly scolds the player for a confrontational response rather than letting the capture demonstrate its result.

Scenes are usually oriented clearly, with intelligible movements between train, depot, museum and tower. Action is easy to picture. Conversely, some decisive events are compressed into report: the bluff sends Buford away immediately; the Authority hears the evidence and supplies a cell; the ending says “Rex Tannen’s trial is short.” Compression is necessary, but it is disproportionately the mechanical and institutional successes that arrive without resistance. Spend a little of the explanation budget on a response, hesitation or exchange at these moments.

**Choices, clues and the actual challenge**

The strongest decision is S23. The tested mounted wheel offers engineering reassurance; the boxed replacement protects the clock’s future but is untested. The earlier P.S. and the story’s concern with preservation support inference without naming the correct object. S25 then permits learning and repair at a cost. This is a good model for the rest of the book: a credible mistaken priority, a consequence that follows it, and an intelligible recovery.

The best-ending investigation has a sound causal chain: chase for access; kiosk for independent authority; log for evidence; museum for Rex’s channel and false story; broadcast for intervention. The reader must actually take action at S42. That is better than a completion checklist automatically cashing out. However, S38 almost gives the final plan verbatim: “We can follow a live public broadcast out of it,” followed by exclusions of recordings and the radio. S42 then offers “Offer him a live interview for the museum.” The connection is real but narrow: match the previously named mechanism to the freshly available answer. The choice’s conditional appearance also supplies an external prompt that the museum knowledge matters now.

This only partly meets the specified level of challenge. Keep both pieces of information, but let one explain a property rather than state the finished tactic. For example, the Authority can establish the need for a public connection reaching its century without enumerating the correct medium and every unsuitable one. The museum can demonstrate Rex’s vanity and channel naturally. At the climax, the player should recognise why appealing to that vanity is useful. Avoid making the wording so cryptic that success becomes a vocabulary test. A reader should be able to explain the connection after choosing, without being told the connection immediately beforehand.

Ordinary success is sensibly accessible. The shop trade is a legitimate solution, and manipulating the catch can save the town without completing an investigation. Those alternatives prevent the best route from becoming the only story worth playing. Still, once the live-interview option exists it has no stated cost or realised downside relative to the ordinary distraction: it can expose Rex, and with the right preparation it catches him. That makes it an attractive extra-reward button. Dramatic uncertainty about Rex’s willingness to cooperate would help; another arbitrary penalty would not.

There are already enough pitfalls. Eight bad endings among eleven endings do not mean the story is particularly difficult, because several are the results of explicitly persevering after catastrophe becomes obvious. “Pull anyway. Better broken than frozen” at S08 follows a warning that everyone will shatter. S25’s refusal follows Doc’s explanation and visibly translucent fingers. E08 requires giving the antagonist the rescue device after he has revealed his priorities. These can remain as reckless alternate outcomes, but they should not be counted as strong strategic dilemmas. Do not remove fair recovery warnings just to disguise them.

The subtler trap is S33’s police report. When the player visited S30k, the danger is properly established. Without that visit, a sensible appeal to law enforcement causes capture. It is recoverable, which substantially reduces unfairness. Nevertheless, S36 reveals the booth’s visible Tannen logo only after the player has committed the evidence; a real observer would see it before handing over the chip. A discreet environmental mention of corporate security earlier in S30 would make this a missed clue instead of withheld observation. It should not become a warning immediately above the police choice.

Trusted adults are treated more fairly than before. Doc corrects dangerous errors, helps when called and does not secretly require the child to distrust him. The independent officer takes evidence seriously. It is legitimate for corporate security to be corrupt within this particular premise. Preserve those distinctions rather than teaching a blanket rule that asking an adult is wrong.

**Side quests, costs and replay value**

The optional content has different kinds of value, which is healthy. S05 supplies atmosphere and confirms an intruder. The chase buys access. S31 combines the strongest speculative scene, the protagonist’s emotional reaction and a later tool. The shop makes the volunteer sacrifice a purchased object while keeping a personal memory. These are not pointless stops, and not every one needs to be mandatory for the best ending.

The kiosk is functional but thin: important world rules arrive in a friendly-face information dump and a compulsory ten-minute promotional video. That joke accounts for the cost, but the player has not chosen to sit through a commercial. A small task of extracting the useful answer from promotional language would give the stop some dramatic texture; this can remain prose, not a new minigame.

The 1885 stationmaster branch needs more payoff. Asking permission brings the player into danger; intervening can save the stationmaster, but he stays under his desk while the player returns to the same crate. Bluffing yields a passing remark from Doc at S26. The radio rescue costs fifteen minutes. Consequently, the optional moral involvement produces spectacle and risk without a lasting human acknowledgement. Stealth is the uncomplicated efficient route, while the story later praises caring about people. A brief response from the stationmaster after a successful intervention would reconcile those values. It need not confer a superior part or make kindness compulsory.

S22’s disguised escort similarly offers a good joke and a consequential temptation, then folds back without a lasting benefit. That is acceptable small-scale colour. The era-order choice supplies pacing control and a couple of contextual callbacks, not a different investigation. Do not describe it as a major alternate plot. Four capture causes in 2085 converge at S36, which efficiently preserves their different arrivals, but most of their subsequent novelty is the same roof rescue. Replay is strongest when comparing the personal shop solution with the museum/evidence route, weaker when collecting several versions of becoming an exhibit.

The biggest problem is how the visible countdown represents those costs. S35’s compact trade and pickup removes seventy minutes; S38’s office visit and pickup removes seventy; S37 removes eighty-five while its prose describes a fifteen-minute cost. The bible explains a seventy-minute base for return/preparation, but the reader does not receive the bible. S26’s trip can plausibly include significant walking and work, whereas the future scenes read as rapid exchanges. S11 explicitly prices a chase at ten minutes even though the described pursuit feels much shorter.

The arithmetic is consistent with the intended balance: core work costs 295, full investigation adds thirty, and Medium’s 35 remaining permits one fifteen-minute recovery while Hard’s twenty permits none for best. The fiction needs to make that arithmetic feel earned. Brief preparation/calibration bridges would suffice; no need to narrate seventy minutes minute by minute. Establish major remaining work early enough that optional detours feel like informed trade-offs.

Likewise, S09 defines zero as the permanent-freeze deadline, but success below twenty produces the missing-minute effect. S41 distinguishes “enough time” only after the assembly cost is spent, and E11 explains the irreversible early tightening afterwards. A player can reasonably believe that finishing before zero is complete success. Seed the clamp’s gradual tightening well before the final choices, without giving a last-second numerical answer. Otherwise E11 risks feeling like an undisclosed grading threshold attached to an excellent eerie image.

**Endings and the quality of the payoff**

E09 is a real victory, with the tower restored and the volunteer entrusted with its care. E10 adds accountability and Doc’s invitation, which fits the partnership. E11’s permanently displaced minute is memorable and specific to this premise. E04’s clock-repair-shop life is an especially good failure epilogue: it preserves the protagonist’s character inside defeat. The non-graphic perils fit a family adventure, though permanent immobilisation and erasure are genuinely frightening; their humour should not erase their emotional consequences.

Four failures return to an exhibit image: E01, E06, E07 and E08. Their causes differ, and E07 gives the museum visit a satisfying dreadful echo, but they offer less replay discovery than the ending count implies. There is no need to add further endings or delete these mechanically. Give the existing ones distinct emotional final beats where worthwhile: missed opportunity, failed bluff, exhausted partnership, betrayal. The different clocks-and-work future in E04 demonstrates the benefit.

Successful aftermath also compresses important differences. S44 without officers exposes Rex publicly, then enters the same E09 as a private escape. E11 can follow Rex’s actual arrest, but its own epilogue concentrates entirely on the minute; it suppresses the uncaught-Rex paragraph rather than giving the caught result a positive acknowledgement. The preceding scene does establish the arrest, so this is not a continuity error. It is a missed chance to make the ending recognise what this player achieved. One conditional sentence for public exposure or arrest would preserve the compact structure.

The anonymous donation resolves the original campaign neatly but from outside the protagonist’s actions. That is an acceptable generous finish, not a plot defect. A tiny moment showing how the new keeper intends to listen to residents would make the final clipboard more than a reward and complete the story’s distinction between stewardship and possession.

**Ranked revision prescriptions**

1. **Must fix before publication: make the countdown trustworthy.** Pages S09, S14, S26, S35, S37–S41, E11. The reader currently cannot reconcile several large drops with the narrated work or anticipate the qualitative penalty before zero. Add short work/travel bridges and early evidence of progressive locking; preserve the tested budgets. Acceptance: a first-time reader can identify what consumed a large block, understands why reserve matters, and does not need the bible to explain an eighty-five-minute deduction beside a fifteen-minute remark.

2. **Must fix to meet the stated challenge brief: leave the final connection to the reader.** Pages S31, S38, S42–S44. Reduce the Authority’s almost verbatim tactical prescription while retaining enough physical information to infer a plan. Acceptance: success requires connecting independently encountered facts; the climax does not simply repeat the earlier answer phrase; an ordinary rescue remains available without investigation. Preserve the improved evidence/broadcast/arrest causality.

3. **High-value polish: complete the human arc and acknowledge different achievements.** Pages S01/S04, S43–S45, E09–E11. Give a rescued person one actual response, and acknowledge broadcast exposure or arrest where relevant. Acceptance: the volunteer’s changed responsibility is visible in an exchange; the ending reflects the player’s major result without replaying the whole climax.

4. **High-value polish: rebalance exposition against experience.** Pages S04, S09, S13–S14. Trim repeated explanatory framing and the dismissive eighty-eight line; retain every rule needed for a fair choice. Acceptance: a newcomer can explain the objective, protection and paradox risk, while the opening reaches its first era change sooner. Reallocate space before expanding the total.

5. **High-value polish: reward the meaning of the stationmaster detour.** Pages S24, S27, S23/S26. Add a small human consequence for successful intervention; remove the narrator’s mild scolding at S28. Acceptance: this route feels like a different moral experience, even when it rejoins the same component quest; bravery need not always succeed or grant an item.

6. **High-value polish: strengthen Rex’s resistance and the police clue.** Pages S30–S31, S33, S36, S44. Plant observable corporate-security information earlier; give Rex a reason to consider the broadcast controllable, followed by a beat in which the protagonist outmanoeuvres that belief. Acceptance: the reporting trap rewards observation, and the final success feels earned against a person rather than arranged by a compliant villain.

Optional matters of taste: one fewer generic science-fiction product name, a little more distinctively imagined 2085 daily life, and greater variation among exhibit epilogues. None justifies new branches, extra franchise cameos or additional punishments. The comic darkness of E04 and E07 should stay.

**Implemented revision — 9 October 2026**

- S04 and S09 replace repeated explanation with observation, a protective intervention and shared deduction. S14 forecasts the remaining work and gives Doc an empirical eighty-eight line. S09 establishes interval damage below twenty minutes well before the finale. Navigation, component preparation and calibration now explain the twenty-, forty-five- and seventy-minute costs; S37 distinguishes its seventy-minute base from its fifteen-minute capture/escape surcharge.
- S38 describes the Authority's need for a public connection to its century without prescribing an interview or eliminating alternatives. S31 demonstrates both the customer channel and Rex's control of it. S42 now offers an introduction to his customers; S44 makes the protagonist overcome Rex's initial refusal and use his permitted question to demand the residents' contracts. - Red answers the volunteer on all successful routes and negotiates quiet nights; E10 records their agreement. A new `rexExposed` flag records the actual broadcast independently of arrest. The kiosk visit now includes the player's persistence in extracting the useful procedure. S30 shows corporate-security branding before the possible police-report trap.
