## Commissioning judgment

All three premises are worth commissioning. None needed a new plot, more endings or a larger cast. At the start of this pass, I would have requested focused revisions before publication: the writing was often vivid and the previous repairs substantial, but some consequences were less convincing than the ideas behind them. The principal weaknesses were overly explicit solutions, incompletely explained costs, uneven detour payoffs and narration that explained a scene's moral meaning after the scene had already demonstrated it.

This is a judgment about editorial quality in a family franchise adventure, not a claim of actual publisher or licensor acceptance. A working audience of confident readers around 10–14, families and nostalgic adults informed the assessment. The project has not supplied a formal publishing age band, and we have not conducted a timed audience study or an external canon clearance.

## Direct answers to the original questions

**Are they engaging?** Yes, with different strengths. Stopped Clocks has the strongest speculative image: becoming an exhibit in the museum of your own failure. Fratelli has the most sustained physical atmosphere and the clearest belonging arc. Return to Sender has the most distinctive relationship between the player's ordinary occupation and the resolution. The moments that weaken engagement generally occur between these strengths: prolonged briefing, skipped chosen actions and repetitive administrative aftermath.

**Too long or too short?** None needs expansion. Before revision, the clean best routes contained 4,771 prose words for Stopped Clocks, 4,289 for Fratelli, and 3,683–3,956 for Return to Sender. Offered choice text adds another 267–457 words on those routes. An illustrative 200–250 words/minute calculation places Stopped Clocks around 20–25 minutes before art and deliberation, and the Fratelli library route around 22–28. The intended 15–25 minute session is therefore tight on some attractive routes. Cut repeated setup and explanation; keep suspense, character action and emotional landings. Return to Sender's six-page commissioned handover is an intentionally short, legitimate resolution, not an undersized version of its full adventure.

**Are they clichéd?** Familiar franchise machinery is appropriate; frictionless execution is the problem. The letter from the past, escaped criminals and unfinished-business haunting are recognizable foundations. Each story gives them an individual purpose. The weaker repetitions are the conveniently cooperative villain, the crowd that keeps misunderstanding after help has arrived, and the efficient information dispenser encountered at each stop. Those need a little resistance, surprise or personality within existing scenes, rather than replacement premises or more lore.

**Do the side quests make sense?** Most do, particularly after the previous revision. A detour can earn its place through practical help, a relationship, a striking scene or knowledge retained after a mistake. It does not have to be a best-ending key. The weakest reviewed examples were the Stopped Clocks stationmaster intervention without a human response, Fratelli's official reports without practical consequence, and Return to Sender's inspection sketch without a distinctive later payoff. The best examples include the museum, the Sloth visit, the wrong-fork latch discovery, the diner and the ghost-trap trade-off.

**Is the happy path too obvious?** Sometimes the visible choice is too obvious while the hidden qualification is too obscure. That combination creates compliance rather than satisfying discovery. An instruction about a live broadcast followed by a button offering a live interview leaves little connection to make. Conversely, learning the release reserve after paying for the return trip prevents an informed decision. The editorial objective is to plant usable evidence early and let the player connect it later. Short, neutral choices can help; merely removing information cannot.

**Enough pitfalls?** Already enough. The reviewed drafts had eight, seven and four bad endings respectively, plus recoverable mistakes and qualified victories. More failures would not automatically make them more challenging. A useful pitfall follows a plausible mistaken priority, has a consequence the reader can understand, and offers recovery where the fiction permits it. Returning the wrong wheel, learning from the wrong drain and correcting a misrouted carrier are stronger designs than adding another obviously foolish fatal button. A permitted refusal of work need not be labelled bad to preserve the ending count.

**Are the choices relevant?** Usually, but their quality varies. Both a mechanical effect and a different emotional experience can make a choice relevant. Some buttons chiefly advance a scene; that is acceptable. The concern is promising an exciting chosen action and skipping its execution, or presenting a tactical decision whose answer is already supplied. The revisions should dramatize the selected action and preserve the consequence, rather than turn every paragraph into a new branch.

**Would they belong in an official tie-in?** Their central ideas, accessible roles and family-adventure tone could. The reviewed drafts still needed a professional finishing pass on voice, earned consequences and emotional payoff. The standard is a satisfying new experience inside the franchise: familiar characters remain recognizable and capable, while the original protagonist contributes something they could not simply delegate away. More cameos, quotes or famous props would not close the identified gap.

## Editorial criteria applied

The review went beyond correctness and plot summary. These are the questions used across the three reports:

| Area | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Opening and promise | Speed of the hook; curiosity; clarity of the player's role; personal investment; whether the opening promises the experience the story delivers. |
| Character | Specificity without a named avatar; an earned arc; protagonist competence and limits; distinct voices; companion agency; ensemble contribution; antagonist or obstacle motivation. |
| Craft | Concrete sensory detail; scene dramatization; dialogue purpose; humor; prose rhythm; exposition load; repeated imagery or moral explanation; clarity of speakers and references. |
| Structure and pace | Length of an actual run; proportion spent preparing; escalation; alternating tension and rest; necessary travel; hub repetition; time allowed for the ending to land. |
| Interactive design | Relevant alternatives; genuine inference; advance information; neutral choice phrasing; plausible mistaken priorities; understandable costs; recoverability; convergence that preserves consequences. |
| Optional content | Causal motivation; practical and emotional payoffs; opportunity cost; retained learning after mistakes; whether an apparent clue matters later. |
| Endings | Differentiated outcomes; proportionate labels; acknowledgment of the actual route; closure of personal as well as practical stakes; replay discovery beyond collecting badges. |
| Franchise and audience | Distinctive franchise pleasure; originality within familiar conventions; newcomer comprehension; family tone; credible adults; respectful characterization; no dependence on recognizing a quotation. |
| Presentation and evidence | Illustration/clue consistency where inspected; meter credibility; measured versus assumed duration; structural checks versus literary judgment; uncertainty that requires actual readers. |

## Revision principles

The previous pass repaired real problems: Doc's competence, Sloth's autonomy, responsible professional handover, meaningful investigations and the ghost-capture accounting. Those repairs stay. The new work should make them feel natural in the fiction, with fewer explanatory certificates and more revealing actions.

The three protagonists should also keep their different emotional centers. Stopped Clocks concerns stewardship rather than ownership. Fratelli concerns belonging through reciprocal friendship. Return to Sender concerns whom a completed job actually serves. Giving every protagonist the same exemplary voice would weaken the collection even if each individual sentence were unobjectionable.

The implementation follows the ranked recommendations in each report. Optional tastes are not automatically requirements: no additional pirate plot, major cameo, new antagonist or collection-wide mechanics are needed. The final review must challenge whether an edit actually makes the story more enjoyable, rather than simply making its intended lesson easier to explain.

## Final commissioning decision

I would take all three revised manuscripts forward to a family tie-in production review. The initial requests for revision have been addressed: the clocks story earns its major deductions and human payoff, the Goonies story makes cooperation and recoverable mistakes credible, and the courier story gives its procedure an individual emotional purpose. The protagonists retain different voices and reasons to matter inside their franchises.

Each final source has an independent, matching continuity stamp. Claude Opus 5.5 re-reviewed the corrected manuscripts; the final results identify **no outstanding continuity defect or material editorial publication blocker**. The follow-up rounds also caught regressions introduced by polishing, including an obscured speaker and a hand-position contradiction. Those were corrected and reviewed again. The reports distinguish accepted corrections from deliberate choices retained after consideration.

This review process has converged: I have no remaining concrete change that I expect to improve the manuscripts without new evidence. A cold-reader session is the useful next test of perceived difficulty, remembered clues, emotional engagement and actual duration—particularly the Stopped Clocks investigation and Goonies library detour. This is an editorial recommendation based on the complete text, reachable routes and the checks described above; it does not represent empirical audience results or an actual publisher's decision.
